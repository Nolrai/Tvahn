Proto-Wind-Tribe (PWT) Phonology and Phonotactics Summary

General Character

PWT is intended to sound:

  • Moderately large in consonant inventory.
  • Rich in codas and short roots.
  • Fairly rugged, with several unusual dorsal and dental sounds.
  • Strongly head-final and isolating, but with enough phonological texture to support later daughter-language divergence.

Distributional Tendencies

  • CVC roots are extremely common.

  • Longer clusters are comparatively rare and therefore perceptually salient.

    • pronouns,
    • particles,
    • postpositions,
    • grammatical markers.

Phonological Character

PWT should sound:

  • compact,
  • consonant-rich,
  • somewhat rough and earthy,
  • suited to riverbanks, storms, reefs, and trade ports,
  • not especially “windy” in its proto stage.

The later association of Wind Tribe cultures with mountains, highlands, and wind symbolism is a historical development rather than an original feature of PWT.

Proto-Windic Grammar

This is meant as an refrence grammer for PWT/Proto-Windic, for now examples are mostly from the Bable translation.

Typological sketch

Proto-Windic is broadly:

  • SOV
  • postpositional
  • topic-first / frame-first
  • pro-drop
  • strongly discourse-particle driven
  • noun-class / classifier heavy
  • comfortable with ellipsis and zero verbs, especially in relative or repeated-clause contexts (To the point that it lacks a coupla.)

2. Basic Clause Structure

PWT is broadly SOV, but this description is somewhat misleading. The language is strongly topic-first, allows pro-drop, and frequently permits verbless clauses. A more useful description is that a clause consists of an optional topic/frame field, followed by a stack of marked argument and adjunct phrases, followed by a clause-final verb complex when one is present.

Schematically:

(to-PHRASE)*  (yayo/i/ro/etc. PHRASES)*  VERB–ADVERBIALS–LINKERS

This is the normal clause skeleton, not a completely rigid slot-filling grammar. For example, adpositional phrases can be fronted, as in many languages, but this produces a marked construction. Compare English “At the store, I will buy a hot dog”: this is grammatical, but less neutral than “I will buy a hot dog at the store.”

Argument and Postpositional Marking

Topic/Frame marker “to”

The topic/frame spot is marked with to (or in certian constructions yayo), and is strongly pefered to be clause first, but the slot can have multiple “to” (TOPIC) phrases in a single clause.

Multiple to phrases normally imply that all of them belong to the same topic/frame, not that the clause has multiple independent subjects.

An empty to phrase, which can be analyzed as a bare discourse particle rather than as a postposition, is used to call attention to the speaker or utterance.

Subject marker yayo

yayo is used when a clause contains both a topic and a separate subject. It also appears in certain constructions, including commands and relative clauses. This is glossed as SUBJ but can be considered more of an non-topic subject / nominative-ish participant / promoted participant.

In relative clauses, the default discourse organization is partly reversed: yayo becomes the unmarked participant marker, while to is used only if a distinct topic must be introduced. How ever the ordering stays to then yayo.

Object and theme markers i and ro

PWT has more than one object-like marker.

i is the ordinary object marker.

ro, glossed here as THEME, is used especially with verbs of holding, bringing, giving, or finding. It can also change the meaning of a verb.

For example:

X i bwax “they see X”

But:

X ro bwax “they find X”

This use of ro developed from longer expressions meaning something like “they saw X and brought news of it.”

In some daughter languages, ro becomes the default direct object marker, while i is lost or restricted to certain verb classes.

Other Postpositional Marking

Genitive xo

xo is a fairly standard possesive marker, much like “of” in English, “de” in Spanish, or “no” in Japanese. Note that befiting PWT’s use of postpositions, the possesor precedes xo and the possessed noun follows it: “X xo Y” means “X’s Y” or “Y of X”. Strings of xo are ambiguous because each xo phrase may either modify the following noun directly or be nested inside the next possessive phrase. That is “X xo Y xo Z” can either mean “Z of X’s Y” or “X, aka Y,’s Z”.

Instramental e

e is an instrumental/source marker. Like one of the meanings of english “with”, however, instead of also having the meaning of “against”, it can be used in phrases where english would use “out of”, “of”, or “from”, such as saying they “made bircks out of clay”, or “they carved the whistle the whistle from wood”.

Locative / relational postpositions

Locative and relational postpositions

PWT uses postpositions for locative and relational meanings such as “in,” “on,” “near,” “under,” “toward,” “from,” and similar spatial or abstract relations. These phrases normally appear in the marked phrase stack before the final verb complex, though they may be fronted when they are topical, contrastive, or otherwise discourse-prominent.

Locative and relational postpositions are not core argument markers in the same sense as to, yayo, i, and ro, but they participate in the same general phrase-stack structure. They can mark physical location, motion, source, goal, accompaniment, or more abstract relations depending on the postposition and the verb.

Specific Postpositional Phrases

Repeated postpositional phrases

PWT allows adjacent phrases headed by the same postposition. These repeated postpositional phrases are often interpreted as parallel, paired, reciprocal, distributive, or appositional constructions.

The fixed phrase:

lipa bo lipa bo one.PERSON TO one.PERSON TO

means:

“one to another” / “to each other”

This belongs to a broader pattern in which repeated postpositional phrases are interpreted according to context.

Reciprocal use

lipa bo lipa bo one.PERSON TO one.PERSON TO

“one to another” / “to each other”

Distributive or chained use

lipa ro lipa ro one.PERSON THEME one.PERSON THEME

“one by one”

Appositional use

shinar ro woŋ ro Shinar THEME lowland THEME

“Shinar, a lowland”

In appositional uses, the repeated phrases are more parallel than their usual English translations suggest. Rather than one phrase simply modifying the other, both phrases present compatible descriptions of the same referent.

Reciprocal possession: lipa-lipa yayo-xo

When a reciprocal relationship would require two different postpositions, PWT can use a contracted reciprocal construction:

lipa-lipa yayo-xo

This is roughly equivalent to an expanded form like:

lipa yayo lipa yayo lipa xo lipa xo

The construction means that each person is paired with another person, rather than with themselves.

By contrast, a simpler phrase such as:

lipa yayo lipa xo

would normally be interpreted reflexively, with the subject and possessor referring to the same person.

## The conjunction ti
ti is a conjunction clitic used to coordinate both clauses and noun phrases. It has three surface forms:
* ti — the full form, used in careful speech and after most consonants * t — a reduced form used after vowels, especially on pronouns * i — a reduced form used after certain consonants: /p t b d tþ ts/
All three forms can coordinate noun phrases or clauses.
However, ti is not used to coordinate postpositional phrases directly. Instead, PWT uses one of two strategies:
1. repeated postpositional phrases 2. coordination inside the phrase, before the postposition
When the first conjunct is a full clause, the second conjunct may replace its verb with a non-reduced ti. In this use, ti is interpreted as repeating or carrying forward the verb from the first clause.
The reduced forms are generally interchangeable with the full form, except in fused or lexicalized forms such as tito, the foregrounding discourse marker.

Commands and cohortatives

Commands are formed with:

  1. the pronoun dlo
  2. the subject marker yayo, marking the commanded agent

A basic command therefore looks like:

dlo yayo xaþt 2ND.COMMAND SUBJ come

“Come!”

The phrase dlo t xya, literally “command-and people,” functions as an inclusive cohortative.

For example:

dlo t xya yayo tlas i gits-i wu sirt 2ND.COMMAND CONJ COLL.PERSON SUBJ brick OBJ make-CONJ completely bake

“Let us make bricks and bake them thoroughly.”

This contrasts with a construction such as:

dlo yayo xya koko [verb]

which commands the addressee to do something with a separate third-party group, rather than including the speaker in the action.

5. Number and exaggeration: tþuwx

PWT uses a base-12 system.

tþuwx literally means 144, but it also functions idiomatically as “a multitude” or “a large number.”

That is why the translation sometimes shifts between literal and idiomatic readings:

tþuwx xya “144 people” → “hundreds / many people / the multitude”

6. Topic marker to

to marks the clause topic or discourse frame. A to phrase usually precedes the main predication and typically comes before explicit subjects or other arguments.

It may mark:

  • the main discourse topic xya to — “as for the people…”

  • a temporal frame [X] xo tþit to — “when / at the time of X…”

  • a conditional or contrastive frame ge kyek to — “without the tower / otherwise…”

7. Clause-initial to

A separate clause-initial to may appear at discourse openings or before especially long or complex topical frames.

This use is not exactly the ordinary topic marker. It functions more like an attention-directing particle, foregrounding the following topic or frame as a single discourse unit.

In constructions that require yayo, the phrase following this attention particle can end in yayo instead of to.

In the opening line of the Babel passage, both uses of to occur together:

to ’ami rol bo tþuwx xya xo ŋewt to lip rolip

The first to is the clause-initial attention particle. The later to marks the actual topical phrase.

8. Subject marker yayo

yayo is used when a clause contains both a topic and a separate subject. It also appears in certain constructions, including commands and relative clauses.

In relative clauses, the default discourse organization is partly reversed: yayo becomes the unmarked participant marker, while to is used only if a distinct topic must be introduced.

10. “The heavens”

The phrase:

tþeŋ ti ’erk rol

literally means something like:

“the black-and-white long things”

but idiomatically means the heavens.

11. Temporal framing with [X] xo tþit

PWT often uses:

[X] xo tþit

literally “the time of X,” as an adverbial temporal construction.

Because PWT strongly disfavors turning verbs into nouns, this is often used where English would say something like “when X was happening.”

For example:

þams xo bwe he kxol xo tþit to “at the time of the inland walking of the families”

Loose English:

“As people moved eastward…”

This construction preserves an ambiguity: it is not fully clear whether “inland” describes the direction of walking, or whether the families were already inland and walking somewhere specific.

12. glam: nursing, settling, inhabiting

glam originally means:

“to nurse / feed milk to, especially babies”

By extension, it also means:

“to settle in, inhabit, cultivate, or make use of a place as a home.”

This semantic extension comes from catgirl life history in the setting. Catgirls travel with infants or while pregnant much less often than humans do, because milk rather than pregnancy is the bottleneck. The birth mother also cannot normally be the nurse mother, because ovaries and breasts are active at different life stages.

So “nursing” becomes a natural metaphor for making a place usable as a home.

13. Consequence and purpose clauses

If/then or so-that relations are expressed with either:

[noun phrase] TOP [verb phrase] MARKER

or:

[clause]. [clause] MARKER

There are three major markers:

  • ’aha — telic marker; the consequence is intended or chosen
  • swuhi — hypothetical marker; “if this is true, then that is true”
  • pasla — consequential marker; the first clause is true, therefore the second follows

Example:

gu-ŋoja to tþuwx xya yayo tlu-ya xo ’ins i nox ’aha “Why? So that many people will say our name.”

Here ’aha marks intended consequence.

14. Directional metaphor: bwebo

bwebo literally means:

“upstream of / on top of / inland from”

It is also used temporally to mean before.

There are two possible metaphors behind this:

  1. upstream as the future or source from which events come toward you
  2. layers deposited one after another, where the top layer is the most recent

The ambiguity is probably still present in PWT.

The related form bwe appears in the physical directional sense in the Shinar line.

15. Object marking: i and ro

PWT has more than one object-like marker.

i is the ordinary object marker.

ro, glossed here as THEME, is used especially with verbs of holding, bringing, giving, or finding. It can also change the meaning of a verb.

For example:

X i bwax “they see X”

But:

X ro bwax “they find X”

This use of ro developed from longer expressions meaning something like “they saw X and brought news of it.”

In some daughter languages, ro becomes the default direct object marker, while i is lost or restricted to certain verb classes.

16. tito

tito is a discourse marker made from the attention particle to plus the conjunction ti.

It is not quite the same as English “but” or “however.” It sits somewhere between:

  • “but”
  • “and then”
  • “but also”
  • “and importantly”
  • “and therefore”

The key point is not necessarily contrast. Instead, tito marks that the next clause is new, important, or narratively foregrounded.

In the Babel passage, it often works well as English “but” because the narrative pivots from human action to divine response.

17. Mass classifier ʃi

ʃi is the mass classifier/counter.

With normally countable nouns, it treats the referent as an uncounted substance, aggregate, or mass rather than as discrete units.

For example:

ʃi-klik

literally means something like:

“house/building as mass”

In this passage, it means the built-up settlement, city mass, or mass of buildings.

18. guwu lix and wu tþit

guwu lix literally means something like:

“unfinished / ongoing thing”

But with the round/singular counter, it is lexicalized as:

“starting point” or “commencement.”

It contrasts with:

wu tþit

literally:

“finished time”

meaning:

“ending-time / completion-time.”

This lets the Babel passage phrase “they have begun to do this” as something closer to:

“behold the starting point of the tower built by the people using one language before now.”

Grammar and usage notes

1. “Say someone’s name”

In PWT, “say someone’s name” can mean to address them, successfully speak with them, or be understood by them.

This lets the Babel passage render the Hebrew idea of “understanding another’s speech” as:

lipa-lipa yayo-xo ’ins i nox-ge “they will not say each other’s names”

This is related to, but distinct from, the earlier use of ’ins i nox, “say a name,” meaning something closer to “make a name known” or “become well known.”

So the same phrase family can cover both:

  • “people will say our name” → we will become famous
  • “they will not say each other’s names” → they will not successfully address / understand one another

2. The conjunction ti

ti is a clause-level conjunction clitic. It has three surface forms:

  • ti — full form, used in careful speech and after most consonants
  • t — reduced form after vowels, especially on pronouns
  • i — reduced form after certain consonants: /p t b d tþ ts/

All three forms can coordinate noun phrases or clauses. However, ti is not used to coordinate adpositional phrases.

When the first conjunct is a full clause, the second conjunct may replace its verb with a non-reduced ti, interpreted as repeating or carrying forward the first clause’s verb.

3. Repeated adpositional phrases

The phrase:

lipa bo lipa bo “one to one” → “to each other”

is a fixed reciprocal phrase. It belongs to a broader pattern where adjacent phrases can be headed by the same postposition.

These constructions can imply several relationships depending on context:

Reciprocal

lipa bo lipa bo “one to another / to each other”

Distributive or chained

lipa ro lipa ro “one by one”

Appositional

shinar ro woŋ ro “Shinar, a lowland”

In appositional uses, the repeated phrases are more parallel than the English translation makes them look.

4. Reciprocal possession and lipa-lipa yayo-xo

When a reciprocal relationship would require two different adpositions, the phrase can contract into a special reciprocal form:

lipa-lipa yayo-xo

This is roughly a contraction of something like:

lipa yayo lipa yayo lipa xo lipa xo

with the narrower meaning that each person is paired with another person.

A simpler phrase like:

lipa yayo lipa xo

would imply the same person, since reflexive interpretation is the default.

5. Number and exaggeration: tþuwx

PWT uses a base-12 system.

tþuwx literally means 144, but it also functions idiomatically as “a multitude” or “a large number.”

That is why the translation sometimes shifts between literal and idiomatic readings:

tþuwx xya “144 people” → “hundreds / many people / the multitude”

6. Topic marker to

to marks the clause topic or discourse frame. A to phrase usually precedes the main predication and typically comes before explicit subjects or other arguments.

It may mark:

  • the main discourse topic xya to — “as for the people…”

  • a temporal frame [X] xo tþit to — “when / at the time of X…”

  • a conditional or contrastive frame ge kyek to — “without the tower / otherwise…”

7. Clause-initial to

A separate clause-initial to may appear at discourse openings or before especially long or complex topical frames.

This use is not exactly the ordinary topic marker. It functions more like an attention-directing particle, foregrounding the following topic or frame as a single discourse unit.

In constructions that require yayo, the phrase following this attention particle can end in yayo instead of to.

In the opening line of the Babel passage, both uses of to occur together:

to ’ami rol bo tþuwx xya xo ŋewt to lip rolip

The first to is the clause-initial attention particle. The later to marks the actual topical phrase.

8. Subject marker yayo

yayo is used when a clause contains both a topic and a separate subject. It also appears in certain constructions, including commands and relative clauses.

In relative clauses, the default discourse organization is partly reversed: yayo becomes the unmarked participant marker, while to is used only if a distinct topic must be introduced.

9. Commands and cohortatives

Commands are formed with:

  1. the pronoun dlo
  2. the subject marker yayo marking the agent

A basic command therefore looks like:

dlo yayo xaþt “come!”

However, the phrase:

dlo t xya

literally “command-and people,” functions as an inclusive cohortative:

dlo t xya yayo tlas i gits-i wu sirt “let us make bricks and bake them thoroughly”

This contrasts with something like:

dlo yayo xya koko [verb]

which would command the addressee to do something with a separate group, rather than including the speaker in the action.

10. “The heavens”

The phrase:

tþeŋ ti ’erk rol

literally means something like:

“the black-and-white long things”

but idiomatically means the heavens.

11. Temporal framing with [X] xo tþit

PWT often uses:

[X] xo tþit

literally “the time of X,” as an adverbial temporal construction.

Because PWT does not strongly favor turning verbs into nouns, this is often used where English would say something like “when X was happening.”

For example:

þams xo bwe he kxol xo tþit to “at the time of the inland walking of the families”

Loose English:

“As people moved eastward…”

This construction preserves an ambiguity: it is not fully clear whether “inland” describes the direction of walking, or whether the families were already inland and walking somewhere specific.

12. glam: nursing, settling, inhabiting

glam originally means:

“to nurse / feed milk to, especially babies”

By extension, it also means:

“to settle in, inhabit, cultivate, or make use of a place as a home.”

This semantic extension comes from catgirl life history in the setting. Catgirls travel with infants or while pregnant much less often than humans do, because milk rather than pregnancy is the bottleneck. The birth mother also cannot normally be the nurse mother, because ovaries and breasts are active at different life stages.

So “nursing” becomes a natural metaphor for making a place usable as a home.

13. Consequence and purpose clauses

If/then or so-that relations are expressed with either:

[noun phrase] TOP [verb phrase] MARKER

or:

[clause]. [clause] MARKER

There are three major markers:

  • ’aha — telic marker; the consequence is intended or chosen
  • swuhi — hypothetical marker; “if this is true, then that is true”
  • pasla — consequential marker; the first clause is true, therefore the second follows

Example:

gu-ŋoja to tþuwx xya yayo tlu-ya xo ’ins i nox ’aha “Why? So that many people will say our name.”

Here ’aha marks intended consequence.

14. Directional metaphor: bwebo

bwebo literally means:

“upstream of / on top of / inland from”

It is also used temporally to mean before.

There are two possible metaphors behind this:

  1. upstream as the future or source from which events come toward you
  2. layers deposited one after another, where the top layer is the most recent

The ambiguity is probably still present in PWT.

The related form bwe appears in the physical directional sense in the Shinar line.

15. Object marking: i and ro

PWT has more than one object-like marker.

i is the ordinary object marker.

ro, glossed here as THEME, is used especially with verbs of holding, bringing, giving, or finding. It can also change the meaning of a verb.

For example:

X i bwax “they see X”

But:

X ro bwax “they find X”

This use of ro developed from longer expressions meaning something like “they saw X and brought news of it.”

In some daughter languages, ro becomes the default direct object marker, while i is lost or restricted to certain verb classes.

16. tito

tito is a discourse marker made from the attention particle to plus the conjunction ti.

It is not quite the same as English “but” or “however.” It sits somewhere between:

  • “but”
  • “and then”
  • “but also”
  • “and importantly”
  • “and therefore”

The key point is not necessarily contrast. Instead, tito marks that the next clause is new, important, or narratively foregrounded.

In the Babel passage, it often works well as English “but” because the narrative pivots from human action to divine response.

17. Mass classifier ʃi

ʃi is the mass classifier/counter.

With normally countable nouns, it treats the referent as an uncounted substance, aggregate, or mass rather than as discrete units.

For example:

ʃi-klik

literally means something like:

“house/building as mass”

In this passage, it means the built-up settlement, city mass, or mass of buildings.

18. guwu lix and wu tþit

guwu lix literally means something like:

“unfinished / ongoing thing”

But with the round/singular counter, it is lexicalized as:

“starting point” or “commencement.”

It contrasts with:

wu tþit

literally:

“finished time”

meaning:

“ending-time / completion-time.”

This lets the Babel passage phrase “they have begun to do this” as something closer to:

“behold the starting point of the tower built by the people using one language before now.”

ʃi is the mass classifier/counter. With normally countable nouns, it treats the referent as an uncounted substance or aggregate rather than as discrete units. Thus ʃi-klik is literally something like “house/building as mass,” here meaning the built-up settlement or mass of buildings.

guwu lix literally means something like “unfinished/ongoing thing,” but with the round/singular counter it is lexicalized as “starting point” or “commencement.” It contrasts with wu tþit, literally “finished time,” meaning “ending-time / completion-time.”
