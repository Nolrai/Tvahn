# Catgirls Timeline — Working Checkpoints

## General Chronological Principle

The Americas and Eurasia are not synchronized. Their relative chronology is roughly inverted compared to OTL.

The American riverine and tropical lowland worlds, especially the Orinoco and later Mesoamerican/Golden-world sphere, develop dense settlement, writing, archives, and classical political traditions earlier than Eurasia.

Eurasia develops more slowly along the urban/literate/state path, but later accelerates through pastoralism, textiles, bronze, iron, trade corridors, and continental military-logistical systems.

“Classical” does not mean bronze-age or iron-age material culture. It means a literate, institutionally remembered world: archives, named historical actors, cities, shrine complexes, diplomacy, offices, legal/ritual memory, and successor traditions.

The present day, c. 2800 ADI, is still far before routine trans-Atlantic navigation. The Americas and Eurasia remain separate historical ecumenes. True trans-Atlantic ships are still roughly 1500 years in the future.

# c. 15,000 BDI — Post-Stabilization Ecology

## Purpose

Establish the stabilized ecological baseline before dense settlement, proto-agriculture, cities, writing, or major built environments.

## Global Ecology

Strong, stable Pacific and Atlantic ocean currents carry heat toward the poles. This warms the planet overall, especially high latitudes, while moderating tropical heat.

Sea levels are higher than during the ATL glacial period. Forests expand and deserts shrink compared both to ATL glacial conditions and to OTL modern conditions.

Antarctica is much warmer than OTL, but still too cold for plants. Ice remains dominant, though ocean-reaching ice sheets are smaller.

## Catgirl Distribution

Catgirls live everywhere except Oceania and Antarctica.

Major-to-minor ratios vary regionally, roughly from 1:3 to 1:7.

## Historical Significance

The world starts with broader belts of productive forest, wetland, coast, river, and estuary than OTL. This creates more places where obligate carnivores can sustain dense populations through fish, shellfish, animal management, forest-edge hunting, and wetland exploitation.

# c. 7,000 BDI — Pre-Urban High-Density Foragers

## Purpose

Show the world before true cities crystallize, when some regions are approaching permanent settlement through dense aquatic and animal-calorie systems.

## Population and Settlement

Population densities are rising in highly productive regions, especially:

* South China / Pearl-Yangzi coastal lowlands
* the Nile
* South American tropical river systems
* Southern Japan
* the American Great Plains
* major estuaries, eel systems, salmon runs, clam reefs, and wetland coasts

Permanent and semi-permanent settlements exist in stable high-density biomes, especially those with predictable aquatic resources.

## Subsistence

Intentional environmental management is widespread, but not yet full agriculture in the human grain-state sense.

Catgirls reshape landscapes through burning, selective planting, animal management, fish traps, wetland modification, coppicing, shellfish management, and controlled access to hunting/fishing grounds.

## Migration

Catgirls reach Australia by this point, or Australian settlement is established by this checkpoint.

## Historical Significance

The pre-urban threshold is not “farming begins.” It is “animal-calorie systems become predictable enough to make permanent settlement viable.”

# c. 5,000 BDI — Orinoco First-City World

## Purpose

Mark the emergence of the first true city-like settlements in the Orinoco system, driven by integrated aquaculture and managed animal production.

## Orinoco River System

Aquaculture and river-pig pens are built into shared multi-use riverbank structures. These systems require and enable permanent settlement along parts of the Orinoco.

Fish, wetland foods, managed river pigs, constructed river-edge infrastructure, and settlement-bound animal flows create one of the first proto-urban zones.

This is not grain urbanism. It is river-engineering, aquaculture, penned animals, and creche/shrine labor coordination.

## Wider Tropical River Systems

Aquaculture develops in many tropical river systems, but in most places it is not yet the staple basis of diet.

The Orinoco is ahead because fish management, animal pens, and built river-edge infrastructure reinforce each other.

## Eurasia

Megalithic structures begin appearing in parts of Eurasia. These do not imply cities or agriculture by themselves. They indicate rising ritual coordination, territorial memory, and labor organization among high-density or seasonally aggregated societies.

## Historical Significance

The first city-world is American and riverine, not Eurasian and grain-based.

# c. 3,500 BDI — Old Orinoco / Eurasian Wetland Emergence

## Purpose

Show the Orinoco as an old multi-city civilization, while Eurasia and North Africa begin forming dense riverine and wetland settlement traditions.

## Orinoco River System

By this point, the Orinoco system has long been occupied by many proto-cities. Small coalitions sometimes rule two or three cities at once, but no polity successfully unifies the whole river system.

The default structure is a patchwork of allied families, rival houses, shrine obligations, foster ties, trade partnerships, and old ritual claims. Many important families have branches in multiple cities, while each city contains several significant families with overlapping external loyalties.

This is close to the default multi-settlement civilizational structure for catgirls:

> one city contains many houses; one house spans many cities.

Political order does not naturally condense into one king, one palace, one capital, or one patrilineal dynasty. It forms through braided networks of creches, senior families, oath-lines, shrine offices, and city councils.

The word glossed as “king” often really means something closer to “ruling family,” “first house,” or “senior creche.” In Sahumank and the Golden Empire, the relevant title literally means “first family of the five-family house.” It is still glossed as “king” because it fills a similar narrative role, but the social dynamics are fundamentally different.

A catgirl “king” is usually not a singular hereditary patriarch. It is more often a ruling household, an office-bearing creche, or the public face of a senior family coalition.

## Eurasia and North Africa

Several Eurasian and North African river systems begin developing dense settlement traditions, but not in the OTL hierarchy.

Strong early candidates include:

* Pearl / lower Yangzi / South China
* Ganges-Brahmaputra / Bengal
* Nile floodplain and delta
* Mekong / Red River
* Mesopotamian marsh-river systems
* Southern Japan and other rich coastal/aquatic zones

These regions support catgirl populations through fish, shellfish, eels, turtles, birds, pigs/river-pigs, wetland hunting, reeds, fodder plants, coppiced woodland, managed edge ecologies, and animal pens.

## Historical Significance

The Nile and Southwest Asia remain important, but they are not uniquely privileged as the first centers of civilization. Wetland productivity matters more than dry grain productivity.

# c. 2,000 BDI — Regional Divergence Across Eurasia / American Urban Maturity

## Purpose

Show divergence between an already old American urban world and still-forming Eurasian regional civilizations.

## Americas

The Orinoco and related tropical river systems are old civilizational landscapes by this point. Their political forms remain braided, multi-city, creche-based, and hard to unify.

Other American regions are developing their own dense food systems, including tropical riverine, coastal, estuarine, and managed forest-edge traditions.

## East and South Asia

South China, the Ganges-Bengal world, Southeast Asian river systems, the Nile, and Mesopotamian marshlands contain dense settlement networks and early urban or proto-urban traditions.

Pearl/Yangzi and Ganges-Bengal are especially strong because obligate carnivore subsistence favors wetland animal-calorie systems: fish, pigs, birds, shellfish, managed fodder, and aquatic edge ecologies.

## Pearl-World Geography

The Pearl/South China coastal world uses a local maritime naming system.

The region conventionally identified with the Pearl River world calls itself something like **“North of the Ocean”**: the mainland coast above the outer saltwater of the South China Sea.

The “ocean” is not the bay or estuary, but the outer saltwater beyond them. The bay, river mouths, tidal flats, and island channels are inner waters; the South China Sea is the true ocean.

Within the same early naming system:

* Vietnam/Tonkin-like regions may be **“East of the Ocean”**
* Yangzi tributary lands may be **“North of the Mountains”**
* the Pearl estuary and bay are inner waters
* the South China Sea is the outer ocean or long sea

These names reflect an early coastal-route worldview, not accurate later cartography. They fossilize when the known world is still limited to the bay, the northern coast of the sea, the turning coast toward Vietnam, and vague inland knowledge beyond the mountains.

## Southwest Asia

The Fertile Crescent, Anatolia, Iranian plateau margins, and Central Asian corridors are increasingly important as pastoral, shrine, exchange, and later metallurgical zones.

They are not yet the world’s obvious civilizational core.

## Historical Significance

This is where the setting’s altered geography becomes obvious: wetland/aquatic worlds are early, while dryland trade and metal corridors become more important later.

# c. 500 BDI — Pastoral and Bird-Textile Disruption

## Purpose

Mark the growth of pastoral systems, animal-fiber systems, bird management, and mobile wealth, especially in Eurasia and northern Africa.

## Grassland and Open Woodland Worlds

Obligate carnivores can exploit open regions very effectively through pastoralism. Herds convert grass, scrub, sedges, mast, and browse into animal calories.

This is especially strong in:

* steppes
* savannas
* floodplain grasslands
* open woodland
* burned parkland
* highland pasture
* semi-arid scrub

## Forested and Wet Regions

Forested pastoralism exists, but it is different from steppe pastoralism.

It relies on browsers, pigs, deer/goat analogues, forest cattle analogues, birds, pannage, mast, fruit, coppice, tubers, wetland plants, and seasonal movement through managed edges.

Dense tropical river forests do not usually support huge open-range herds. Their high-density package is instead:

> aquaculture + penned wetland animals + managed forest edge.

Burning and cutting are used selectively to create productive edge mosaics: clearings, coppiced groves, fruit/nut stands, managed marshes, fish ponds, river-pig pens, canal edges, bright-light fodder patches, and fallow browse gardens.

## Historical Significance

Pastoralism becomes a major force, but it does not produce one universal pathway. Steppe pastoralists, forest pannage societies, river-pen cultures, and wetland aquaculture states all diverge.

# c. 0 ADI — Isama / Golden / Early Classical Snapshot

## Purpose

Introduce the early classical American world: literate, urban, shrine-centered, politically self-conscious, and historically remembered.

## Mesoamerica / Golden World

The Golden/Mesoamerican world is entering or already within its classical phase.

It has:

* writing
* archives
* named historical actors
* formal offices
* shrine complexes
* urban centers
* regional diplomacy
* ritual-political law
* remembered founders and dynastic/creche traditions
* successor cultures that reinterpret earlier figures

This is classical because it is literate and institutionally remembered, not because bronze or iron is central.

## Isama

Isama belongs to this early classical historical world. She is important in something like a George Washington sense: a real political figure whose later memory becomes much larger, stranger, and more mythically/theologically loaded than her immediate historical role.

Later occult, minority religious, proto-nationalist, and successor traditions reuse and reinterpret her, sometimes after memory of her original nation has faded.

## Eurasia

The strongest Eurasian regions are closer to early monumental or early state-forming horizons. Some have proto-writing or early writing; others have large ritual landscapes, dense settlements, food-storage systems, and shrine-centered administrations.

## Historical Significance

The Americas are old; Eurasia is still crystallizing.

# c. 500 ADI — End of Classical Golden Empire / Eurasian Monumental-State Horizon

## Purpose

Mark the end or transformation of the Classical Golden Empire while Eurasia reaches something closer to pyramid-era or early dynastic monumental civilization.

## Golden Empire

The Classical Golden Empire ends, fragments, or transforms into successor worlds.

Its archives, shrines, political vocabulary, origin stories, and remembered figures remain powerful. Later societies define themselves through inheritance, rejection, reinterpretation, or restoration of Golden-world legitimacy.

## Americas

The wider American world is not unified, but it is deeply historical. Multiple literate or archive-using traditions exist or interact with Golden-world prestige.

## Eurasia and North Africa

The Nile and Southwest Asia are closer to an early monumental-state horizon: large labor systems, sacred land offices, shrine-kingship, food-storage administration, early writing or proto-writing, and monument building.

South China, Ganges-Bengal, Southeast Asian lowlands, and marsh Mesopotamia remain major high-density zones.

## Oceanic State

The Atlantic is still closed as a civilizational barrier. There is no routine trans-Atlantic contact.

## Historical Significance

500 ADI Mesoamerica is classical. 500 ADI Eurasia is closer to Great Pyramid / early monumental-state levels in the strongest regions.

# c. 1000 ADI — Golden Successor Worlds / Southwest Asian Bronze Breakthrough

## Purpose

Mark the growing importance of bronze in Southwest Asia and Eurasian corridors, while the Americas remain post-classical and institutionally old.

## Americas

Golden successor worlds continue to develop. Their politics are shaped by old archives, shrine inheritance, creche legitimacy, trade, regional conflict, and competition over classical memory.

Bronze, where present in American contexts, is important but not civilization-founding.

In Mesoamerican/Golden-world contexts, bronze affects:

* prestige goods
* ritual sound objects
* bells and resonant metals
* elite tools
* craft specialization
* tribute
* portable wealth
* shrine display
* trade networks

It does not create cities, writing, states, or imperial ideology. Those already exist.

## Southwest Asia / Eurasia

Bronze becomes more disruptive here because it combines with:

* pastoral mobility
* trade corridors
* elite weapon production
* woodworking and transport
* shrine wealth
* competitive state formation
* long-distance exchange
* portable prestige goods

## Historical Significance

Bronze is not equally revolutionary everywhere. In the Americas it is a late classical or post-classical intensification. In Southwest Asia and Eurasia it is closer to a system-changing pressure.

---

# c. 1500 ADI — Bronze Diffusion and Regional Divergence

## Purpose

Show bronze spreading unevenly and interacting differently with already distinct regional systems.

## Eurasia

Bronze diffuses through trade corridors, pastoral networks, shrine economies, and emerging states.

Its impact varies by ecology:

* Steppe and open woodland zones use it for weapons, fittings, prestige, and mobile elite exchange.
* River states use it for tools, ritual goods, administration, and elite display.
* Mountain and corridor zones gain importance through ore access and metalworker mobility.
* Wetland states incorporate metal into already dense aquatic/animal economies.

## Americas

American civilizations remain institutionally older. Bronze may spread in some places, but it is not the main historical divider.

Some American regions may use bronze heavily for ritual, elite craft, and portable wealth while continuing to rely on obsidian, chert, bone, wood, fiber, hide, cotton armor, and organic technologies for many practical purposes.

## Historical Significance

The world is increasingly connected within hemispheres, but not globally. Metallurgy creates sharper regional differences without producing a single universal “Bronze Age.”

# c. 2000 ADI — Late Bronze / Early Iron Patchwork

## Purpose

Mark the emergence of a patchwork world where iron appears in some regions while bronze remains important elsewhere.

## Eurasia

Eurasia becomes much more dynamic. Iron begins appearing or spreading unevenly, especially where ore access, charcoal, trade routes, and state demand align.

Bronze remains important, especially for elite display, ritual, fittings, and places where bronze-working systems are entrenched.

Eurasian state systems are now more competitive, more militarized, and more connected than before.

## Americas

The American world is old, literate, post-classical in many regions, and politically diverse. Some areas may have mature successor states, religious leagues, shrine federations, old archive cultures, and regional imperial attempts.

Metal is important, but American political history is not organized around metallurgy in the same way.

## Oceans

The Atlantic is still a barrier. Trans-Atlantic ships are not present. Interhemispheric contact, if any, is mythic, accidental, or so rare as to be historically negligible.

## Historical Significance

By this point, Eurasia is accelerating hard, while the Americas possess deeper institutional age and older written memory.

---

# c. 2500 ADI — Mature Iron States / Pre-Imperial Pressure

## Purpose

Show mounting pressure toward large states and continental-scale polities.

## Eurasia

Mature iron states emerge in several regions. The combination of iron, pastoral systems, trade corridors, textiles, animal transport, and administrative writing creates larger and more aggressive political formations.

Southwest Asia, the Nile, South China, Ganges-Bengal, and Mediterranean/coastal regions all become major players, but not in a single synchronized pattern.

## Americas

American successor systems remain highly developed. Their political forms may be older, more archive-heavy, more shrine-legitimist, and more creche-networked than Eurasian states.

Some American polities are continental in ambition, but geography, ecology, and braided family/city structures complicate stable territorial empire.

## Historical Significance

This is the age of pre-imperial pressure: states are getting larger, military systems are hardening, and old local structures are being forced into larger political frames.

# c. 2800 ADI — Present Day / First Continental Empires

## Purpose

Set the present-day world: first continental empires are emerging, but the world is still pre-Atlantic and not early-modern.

## Global Situation

The present day is roughly equivalent in broad technological-historical feel to an Iron Age / early imperial-pressure world, not to the Age of Discovery.

It has:

* literate archives
* old classical traditions
* mature iron in key regions
* bronze still important in many places
* long-distance overland and coastal trade
* major religious-political traditions
* continental empires beginning to form
* regional imperial ideologies
* professional or semi-professional military systems
* old shrine networks and city leagues
* no routine trans-Atlantic navigation

## Americas

The Americas are institutionally old. Mesoamerican/Golden-world successor cultures preserve deep archives, classical memory, shrine legitimacy, and old political vocabulary.

The American world may have the oldest continuous literate traditions.

## Eurasia

Eurasia has caught up in many military, metallurgical, and state-building terms. It may even be overtaking some American regions in raw expansionary pressure because iron, pastoral mobility, trade corridors, and competitive state systems reinforce each other.

But Eurasia’s literate state traditions are younger.

## Oceans

The Atlantic remains a real civilizational boundary.

Routine trans-Atlantic shipping is still roughly 1500 years away from the present day. This means no Atlantic colonization, no global oceanic empires, no hemispheric exchange, and no 1492-equivalent world-system.

## Historical Significance

The present-day anchor is:

> The Americas are old, Eurasia is accelerating, and the oceans are still closed.
