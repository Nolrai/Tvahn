The conjuction has three forms a full form "ti" used in careful speech and after consonants other than (p t b d tþ ts), and a "t" form used after vowels, especially on pronouns, and an "i" only used after (p t b d tþ ts). All three of these can coordinate NP or clauses, but is not used to coordinate adpositional (a.k. PP) phrases.

When the first conjuct is a clause, the second conjunct can replace the verb with a (nonreduced) 'ti' , interpreted as replication of the first clause's verb.

'lipa bo lipa bo' is a fixed phrase meaning "one to another", "to each other", or "one to one". It is an example of a broader rule in which adjacent phrases may be headed by the same prepositions.. Such constructions have somewhat ambiguous semantics and may imply:

pairing or reciprocal relations (lipa bo lipa bo "to each other"),
chained or distributive interpretations (lipa ro lipa ro "one by one"),
or appositional descriptions, where multiple phrases describe the same referent (shinar ro woŋ ro "Shinar, a lowland").

In appositional uses the repeated phrases are more parallel than their usual English translations.

'to' (topic marker)

'to' marks the clause topic or discourse frame. The to-phrase precedes the main predication and typically comes before explicit subjects and other arguments. It may mark:

* the main discourse topic (xya to “as for the people…”)
* a temporal frame ([X] xo tþit to “when / at the time of X…”)
* a conditional or contrastive frame (ge kyek to “without the tower / otherwise…”)

clause-initial 'to'

A separate clause-initial to may appear at discourse openings or before especially long or complex topical frames. This functions as an attention-directing particle, foregrounding the following topic/frame as a single discourse unit. In constructions that demand the use of 'yayo' the phrase following this use of 'to' can end in 'yayo' instead of 'to'.

In the opening line of the Babel passage, both 'to' occur together (as they usually will): the first to is the clause-initial attention particle, while the later 'to' marks the topical phrase itself.

'yayo' (Subject marker)
'yayo' is used when either there is both a topic and and a subject in the same clause, or in certian constructions (such as commands, and relative clauses),

In relative clauses, the default discourse organization is reversed: yayo becomes the unmarked participant marker, while to is used only when a distinct topic must be introduced.

Commands are formed by 1) using the pronoun dlo, and 2) using the yayo postposition to mark the agent. Usually this means dlo is part of the NP that gets marked with yayo.

In command constructions, 'dlo t xya' literally “command-and people” functions as an inclusive cohortative, unlike "dlo yayo xya koko VERB", which commands the addressee to do the verb _with_ a seperate group.

'ro' (glossed as THEME) is a direct object marker used for holding, bringing, or giving. Using it can often change the meaning of a verb. "X i bwax" means "[they] see the X", "X ro bwax" means "[they] found the X", though with other verbs you just can't use "i" or must use "i". The verb meaning modifying uses derived from longer phrases meaning "they saw X and brought [news of] it". In some daughter languages 'ro' becomes the default direct object marker, with "i" either being lost or being resticted to certian verb classes.

'tito' - discourse marker, tito is composed of the attention getter 'to' and the conjunction 'ti' and isn't quite a contrastice like "however" or "but" but is rather somewhere between that, and "and then" or "but also", the key point is not that the second clause is unexpected, but that it is new and interesting, or important. So can also be used where Enlgish would use "and therefor:" but here it does serve to say "new event!".

ʃi is the mass classifier/counter. With normally countable nouns, it treats the referent as an uncounted substance or aggregate rather than as discrete units. Thus ʃi-klik is literally something like “house/building as mass,” here meaning the built-up settlement or mass of buildings.

guwu is an adverb expressing an "unfinished" or imperfective mood, its composed of "gu" a negative marker and "wu" which means completely/thoughly/finished.
