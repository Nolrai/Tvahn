# The Isama Revolution

## Who Was Isama?

Isama has been an important figure in the religious and occult traditions of Central America’s catgirls for almost three millennia. For much of that time, scholars argued over which empire, religion, or culture she had “truly” belonged to. Was she a Golden Saint, a stock character from Wind Tribe drama, an incarnation of the Lamp Goddess, or an alchemical metaphor? Modern research has increasingly concluded that she was originally something both more mundane and, arguably, more consequential.

The growing consensus identifies Katik Isama as a war-leader at the center of the crisis that created what archaeologists now call Monumental Sahumank, on the shores of the lake known in human timelines as Lake Nicaragua. At this time the cities on the lake shore had been at war with the expanding Golden Empire for multiple generations. Isama's self-sacrifice and command to obey her rival resolved the crisis by transforming the office of katik (/gaɾix/), previously a title for an experienced military leader, into the head of a single permanent Sahumank military organization.

Katik Isama's self-sacrifice, taking on the role and fate of a prisoner of war while at the hight of her power, gave a spiritual, even divine strength to her final command, ordering her forces to obey her rival, Katik Salima /Zaɾim/. This was vital to actually resolving the crisis and giving Salima the authority needed to both allow some soldires to loot the food they needed to survive, and condem others to not getting enough to recover from battle.

Sahumank’s soldiers had been promised more food, land, and wealth than the lake side cities could provide. The problem was not the expected one of a failed military campaign, that could be resolved by rewarding small successes and letting those who failed pay the price, but that of an unusually successful season: they had driven back the invading Golden Empire, captured forts, and marched into its border settlements, only to find villages and farmland burned or abandoned. The empire recalled its troops, collapsed its strongpoints, and reduced others to skeleton crews with limited supplies. Because the conquered borderlands had depended upon a continuous imperial flow of soldiers, equipment, organization, and farmers, Sakumank's advancing armies found the wealth and food was scattered, destoried, or simply never produced or transported there in the first place.

Before Isama's actions the three Katik strugled to restrain their soldires from turning as a whole to loot the cities of Sahumank, which would ultimately distory the very systems they needed to survive the next winter. While this new authority did not instantly translate to peace or stability, it did consolidate two of the three factions, and create a single office that mere respect, oratory, or martial skill was not enough to challenge. This consolidation and the following expultion of both the remaining faction (Katik Steta /dʒeɾa/), and enough of their own forces was enough to reduce the military food demand down to what the scattered remaining farmsteads and the (now "victorious") lakeside cities could support without entering a cycle of collapse. If the wet season had been too long, or the dry season too dry this still could have failed, but it didn't and the new political order surived, and eventually prospered.

Its unclear what exactly happened to the exiled troops, and enough arived at the edges of the Golden Empire to create a sort of buffer zone of starvation and violence, distinct archelogically from that conqured as part of the organized campaign. Others probably succeded at fleeing north or east to the less setled comunities of what would eventually become the Wind Tribe Confederacy, including the ones around the Lamp Goddess's Shrine that many of them hailed from.

Sacrafice of POWs, the corpses of fallen commrads, and even captured loot, was already a core part of the warrior cult of the Lamp Goddess, but the willing self immolation of Isama started a shift from, the at the time, commom place role of ritual purity and wealth power display (and also a way to keep soldires moving as part of an campain, and not stay at an victory site), into a more mystical-spiritual practise of oath creation and self-denial or injury.

That organization then needed to record its founding, and used the newly created meathod of carving writing into stone to do so, creating the the proliferation of writen inscriptions accompining and replacing earlier "tar bird" style temple construction that defines Monumental Sahumank. Which then later shades into Classical Sahumank, where painted and stone carved lables and signs become common, but large scale building projects become rare.

All three periods involve large organized conflict with the Golden Empire to the south over the lands reached by the rivers going south and east from the lake, peaking in inintensity just before the Isama crisis, and at the end of the Classical Sahumank period when the Sahumank polity was partitioned by The Golden Empire, and the Wind Tribe Confederacy.

# Unintegrated content:

First it is important to distinguish three different broader collections that both bear Isama's name in modern scholarship from the particular revolution we are talking about here. The Isama Cycle is the name given to modern reconstructions of the Sahumank national narrative, large portions of which predate or postdate Isama's life by hundreds of years. Isama's rise and death is only the first discovered and most repeated portion of this cycle. And then there is the hundreds of stories that draw on Isama as an Golden Saint, or Wind Tribes stock character, and accompianing religious and spiritual texts that interpreted and reinterpreted those texts as spiritual lessions, grimorirs, or prophesy. None of the cultures involved had a distinction between historian and prophetess, or economist and priest, seeing the world as made up of a dense tapastry of spirits, omens, gods, and wisewommen.

So while in some sense the earliest versions are more 'historical' or 'mundane' than the later liturature that is fundamentally a modern division that would have been alien to the writers.

Many intepretations of the last few centuries tried to metaphorise or soften her sacrafice into maryterdom or bravery. However the older tales are quite explicit that she ritually immolated her self, and in doing so died, this was clearly the central part of her importance in a moral sense. In those early stories the point is not that sentient sacrafice was done, it was that she sacraficed herself. Sacraficing the bodies of warriors that fell in battle and prisoners of war was a core part of the Lamp Goddess's worship at the time, and while the Lamp Goddess worship seems to have emphasised it more then other warrior cults in the region, it was standard war time practice, many schollars point out that given the prevelence of disease, parasites, and the quick speed of rot, burning battlefields, bodies, and prisoners would have been an effective way to limit the spread of disease.

But ultimately it was her political actions, what she is said to have commanded her troops to do _after_ her death that cemented her as the hinge on which a revolution in the political structure of Sahumank and the seeds of the later post-imperial religions of Central and South America. By ordering her loyalists to serve her rival she created a new standard of legitimacy divorced from either the urban shrine building of the lakeside city Five Families, or pure martial success.
